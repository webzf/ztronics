## Where the Arduino UNO Q fits

The UNO Q is not a classic starter kit: it is a single board that pairs a Linux-capable Qualcomm Dragonwing QRB2210 (quad-core Cortex-A53 at 2 GHz) with an STM32U585 microcontroller for real-time control, in the familiar UNO form factor. You can write classic sketches, code in Python on the Debian Linux side, or mix both in Arduino App Lab. It comes in a 2 GB / 16 GB version and a 4 GB / 32 GB version.

- **Choose it if** you already know the basics, or want to work with cameras, audio, local AI models or a Linux desktop alongside sensors and motors.
- **Skip it as a first board if** you just want to learn circuits and C++ sketches. It costs more than an UNO, and reviewers note that the 2 GB version is sluggish as a desktop computer.
- **Best pairing:** start with one of the kits above for the parts and fundamentals, then add a UNO Q for the project that needs Linux or AI.

The UNO Q is sold as a board, so buy sensors, a breadboard and cables separately, or reuse the parts from a starter kit.

## How to choose

- **Your experience.** Complete beginner: Plug and Make or the official Starter Kit. Some experience: Elegoo for parts, UNO Q for bigger ideas.
- **How you like to learn.** A printed, project-based book favors the official kit; quick results and apps favor Plug and Make.
- **Budget.** Elegoo is the cheapest per component. Official kits cost more but come with polished guides.
- **Board generation.** UNO R3 kits are the best documented, UNO R4 WiFi adds Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, and UNO Q adds Linux and AI.
- **Where you want to go.** Circuits and electronics: breadboard kits. IoT dashboards: R4 WiFi. Vision, audio and edge AI: UNO Q.
- **Age and supervision.** Elegoo lists its kit for ages 12+. Younger learners do well with Plug and Make alongside an adult.
